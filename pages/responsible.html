Our Commitment

Stewardship Starts With You

The Pacific Northwest's trails are treasures shared by everyone. With growing visitation, our collective responsibility grows too. Small actions — staying on trail, packing out trash, keeping noise low — add up to big impact when thousands of hikers practice them.

These aren't just rules. They're principles that keep wild places wild.

Leave No Trace

Seven Core Principles

Click any principle to expand detailed tips for hiking in the Pacific Northwest.

1

Plan Ahead & Prepare

Good planning prevents emergencies and reduces environmental impact. Know the regulations and conditions before you go.

Check trail conditions and weather forecasts before departing
Download offline maps — cell service is unreliable in the Cascades
Research permit requirements (many popular trails require advance booking)
Tell someone your itinerary and expected return time
2

Travel on Durable Surfaces

Staying on established trails is the single most impactful thing hikers can do to protect backcountry ecosystems.

Walk single file through the middle of the trail — even when it's muddy
Never shortcut switchbacks — it causes severe erosion
Camp at least 200 feet from lakes, streams, and trails
On alpine terrain, step on rock or snow, never fragile vegetation
3

Dispose of Waste Properly

Pack out everything you pack in — including food scraps, orange peels, and apple cores (they don't decompose as fast as you think).

Carry a small trash bag — pick up litter you find too
Bury human waste in a cathole 6–8 inches deep, 200 ft from water
Pack out all toilet paper and hygiene products
Use biodegradable soap and wash dishes 200 ft from any water source
4

Leave What You Find

Take only photographs, leave only footprints. Natural and cultural objects are part of the ecosystem and belong where they are.

Don't pick wildflowers, berries, or remove rocks and wood
Leave historical and cultural artifacts untouched
Don't build cairns, shelters, or trenches
Avoid introducing or transporting non-native species
5

Minimize Campfire Impacts

Many areas in the Pacific Northwest prohibit campfires in summer due to wildfire risk. Always check current regulations.

Use a lightweight camp stove instead of a fire when possible
Only use established fire rings where fires are permitted
Keep fires small — only burn sticks you can break by hand
Ensure fires are completely cold before leaving camp
6

Respect Wildlife

The PNW is home to bears, cougars, elk, and countless other species. Give them space — for your safety and theirs.

Observe wildlife from a distance — never approach or feed animals
Store food in a bear canister or hang it 10 ft up and 4 ft from a tree
Keep dogs on leash — they can trigger wildlife encounters
Make noise on the trail to avoid surprising bears
7

Be Considerate of Other Visitors

Everyone on the trail deserves a quality experience. A little courtesy goes a long way.

Yield to uphill hikers — they have right of way
Step aside to let faster parties pass safely
Keep music to yourself — use headphones or keep it silent
Keep group conversations at a natural speaking volume

Quick Reference

Do's & Don'ts on the Trail

The most common mistakes hikers make — and how to avoid them.

✅ Do This

💧Carry more water than you think you need — at least 0.5L per hour
📍Stay on the marked trail, especially in alpine and meadow zones
🐾Keep dogs on a 6-foot leash at all times
🗑️Pack out all trash, including food scraps and peels
📣Make noise in bear country — talk, clap, use a bear bell
☁️Turn around if weather deteriorates — no summit is worth your safety

❌ Don't Do This

🚫Don't feed wildlife — it makes them dependent and aggressive
🚫Don't play music out loud — it disturbs others and scares wildlife
🚫Don't cut switchbacks — even if the trail looks long. It causes erosion.
🚫Don't pick wildflowers or remove rocks, even as souvenirs
🚫Don't approach or get between a mother animal and her young
🚫Don't leave the trailhead without telling someone your plans

Trail Etiquette

Sharing the Trail

Simple rules that make trails better for everyone.

⬆️

Yield to Uphill Hikers

Hikers going uphill have the right of way. Step aside and let them pass — they're working harder and need momentum.

🐴

Horses Have Priority

Horses and pack animals have the highest right of way. Step to the downhill side, speak calmly, and wait until they've passed.

🚵

Cyclists Yield to Hikers

On multi-use trails, mountain bikers yield to hikers. But be safe — step aside if a fast rider is approaching.

🔇

Keep Noise Down

Headphones if you want music. Nature sounds are why people come — don't deprive others of that experience.

📸

Don't Block the Trail

Taking photos? Step off to the side. Don't stop in the middle of the trail, especially on narrow sections or switchbacks.

🐕

Responsible Dog Hiking

Keep dogs leashed, carry poop bags and use them, and ask before letting your dog approach other hikers or their pets.

Wildlife Safety

Pacific Northwest Wildlife Guide

What to do if you encounter these animals on the trail.

🐻

Black Bear

Use Caution

Make noise to avoid surprising them. If you see one, stay calm, speak firmly, and back away slowly. Never run. If attacked, fight back.

🦁

Cougar (Mountain Lion)

Stay Alert

Rarely seen but present. If encountered, make yourself large, maintain eye contact, and back away. Never run or crouch down.

🦌

Elk & Deer

Keep Distance

Beautiful but unpredictable, especially during rut (fall) and when calves are present. Stay at least 50 feet away and don't approach.

🐍

Rattlesnake

Stay Alert

Found in dry, rocky areas below 5,000 ft. Watch where you step and put your hands. If bitten, stay calm and get to hospital immediately.

🦅

Eagles & Owls

Enjoy Safely

Common in the PNW. Observe from a distance — never approach nests. Protected by federal law. Keep dogs away from nesting areas.

🐿️

Marmots & Pikas

Enjoy Safely

Adorable alpine residents. Never feed them — human food disrupts their hibernation cycles. Watch and photograph from a distance.

Be Prepared

Emergency Preparedness

Know what to do before something goes wrong. Preparation saves lives.

🆘 If You Get Lost

  • Stop, stay calm, and don't panic
  • Stay put — rescuers look where you were last seen
  • Use a whistle: 3 blasts = universal distress signal
  • If you must move, follow water downhill to civilization
  • Use a signal mirror or bright clothing to attract attention

🤕 If Someone Is Injured

  • Keep the injured person still and warm
  • Call 911 if you have cell signal — give GPS coordinates if possible
  • Send two people for help; never leave someone alone
  • Use a first aid kit for wounds, blisters, or sprains
  • For suspected fractures: splint and don't move the joint

⛈️ Caught in Bad Weather

  • Turn around early — don't summit if weather is closing in
  • Seek shelter below treeline in a lightning storm
  • Avoid lone trees, ridges, and open water in lightning
  • For hypothermia: get dry, add layers, share body heat
  • For severe weather: deploy emergency bivy and wait it out
