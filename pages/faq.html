Frequently Asked Questions

Everything you need to know before hitting the trail.

Getting Started

Use the filter buttons on our Trails page to browse by difficulty — Easy, Moderate, or Hard. Each trail card also shows the total distance and estimated time so you can plan accordingly. If you're new to hiking, start with an Easy trail under 3 miles.
All distances listed on this site are round trip unless otherwise noted. So a trail listed as 4.0 miles means 2 miles each way.
Hidden Gem trails are lesser-known routes that offer outstanding scenery or experiences but tend to see fewer visitors than popular trails. They're great options if you want to avoid crowds.

Safety & Preparation

The Ten Essentials for any day hike: navigation (map/GPS), sun protection, insulation (extra layers), illumination (headlamp), first-aid kit, fire starter, repair tools/knife, nutrition (extra food), hydration (extra water), and emergency shelter. Always tell someone where you're going and when you expect to return.
Check the Washington Trails Association website for recent trip reports, which are updated by hikers after their visit. Also check the Washington State Recreation and Conservation Office site for permit and closure information.
Stay calm and give animals plenty of space. Never feed wildlife. For bears: make noise while hiking, carry bear spray, and know how to use it. For cougars: do not run, make yourself look large, and back away slowly. Always observe wildlife from a safe distance.

Permits & Passes

Many Washington trailheads require a Discover Pass ($30/year) or a Northwest Forest Pass ($30/year). Some parks require separate day-use fees. We recommend checking the specific trailhead requirements before heading out — details are on each trail's detail page.
Dog policies vary by trail and land management agency. In general, dogs must be on a leash 6 feet or shorter on all public lands. Some areas, such as national park trails, prohibit dogs entirely. Always check trail-specific rules before bringing your pet.

Leave No Trace

The seven principles are: Plan Ahead and Prepare, Travel and Camp on Durable Surfaces, Dispose of Waste Properly, Leave What You Find, Minimize Campfire Impacts, Respect Wildlife, and Be Considerate of Other Visitors. Learn more on our Hike Responsibly page.
If no restroom is available, dig a cat hole 6–8 inches deep, at least 200 feet (70 adult steps) from water, trails, and camp. Pack out all toilet paper in a sealed bag, or use natural materials and pack them out. In high-use alpine areas, a WAG bag (waste alleviation and gelling) is required — these are available at ranger stations.
